5The sites

The places are where he was; these are what he built, and what became of it. 13 structures are recorded — the island establishment on Hven and the Bohemian dwellings of the exile — each with its date, its survival status, the rooms it contained and the instruments they housed, its fate, and, where one exists, a link to the thing itself. The Hven register is in the order of building: the house begun in 1576, the laboratory and the library in 1580, Stjerneborg and the printing house in 1584, the paper mill in 1589. For Uraniborg and Stjerneborg the primary architectural record is Charles Christensen's measured reconstruction of 1921, printed here with its excavation evidence, its dimension comparison and the surviving relics; Blaeu's much-reproduced views are given beneath it as the inherited print tradition they are, schematic and unreliable in detail. The depictions of Uraniborg are compared in Imagines; the island's visitors are in Diarium.

Hven — the island establishment 7 sites

Uraniborg — Tycho's house and observatory, 1576–1597

ruinedA1576–1580 · Ven (Hven)site-uraniborg
Tycho's great house, built 1576–1580 ön a square rampart-enclosed plot whose four corners pointed to the compass points, with four roads running from those corners to the raised circular space at the centre where the house stood. The central building was a square 49 feet ön a side, with round towers 18 feet in diameter ön its north and south faces (ringed by lower outhouses för fuel) and narrow entrance towers east and west; it was built apparently of red brick with sandstone ornaments in the 'Gothic Renaissance' style then new to the North — epoch-making, Dreyer judged, in the history of Scandinavian architecture. A single building uniting residence, library and upper observatory galleries above än alchemical laboratory in the basement. Its artistic genesis is still debated — advisers proposed from Hans van Paeschen (first builder of Kronborg) to the royal sculptor Johan Gregor van der Schardt — but the 1587 tablet in the great mural-quadrant painting names Tycho himself 'the constructor of the buildings and the astronomical instruments', and the domed-centre-in-a-geometric-garden plan shows a strong affinity with Italian villa architecture, especially Palladio's Villa Rotonda (Johannsen, in Tycho Brahes Verden, 2006). The standard art-historical monograph ön the building — Jern's Uraniborg: herresäte och himlaborg (Lund, 1976) — locates the specific model för the gabled façade not in the villa tradition but in Venetian church fronts, comparing it directly with S. Giovanni in Bragora and S. Maria della Carità, while cautioning that Uraniborg cannot be reduced to any single Renaissance style-circle (Jern 1976). The documented hands are these: the Netherlandish master-builder Hans von Paschen led the work before transferring to Bohus in 1577; Hans van Stenwinkel the Elder completed the interior and built Stjerneborg (royal architect, 1582); the Nuremberg founder Georg Labenwolf laid the lead-pipe water system and fountain; and Tobias Gemperlin painted the great mural quadrant (Jern 1976). Over its two doors Tycho set paired mottoes that made the building's double programme explicit — Suspiciendo despicio ('by looking up I look down') över the eastern, astronomical portal, and Despiciendo suspicio ('by looking down I look up') över the western, alchemical one — so that astronomy and the laboratory's 'terrestrial astronomy' answered each other across the house (Jern 1976). In his own words, Tycho chose “the site highest ön this island … practically in its centre, where I started building from its foundations a castle which was named after the heaven itself in which it was its task to make observations, and hence called Uraniborg.” He gives the rampart-square as three hundred feet ön a side, the central house as “nearly sixty feet” per side under a forty-five-foot wall, and seventy-five feet “from the ground to the Pegasus at the very top” (Mechanica, trans. Ræder, Strömgren & Strömgren 1946, p. 126). These figures are in Tycho's short foot of 259 mm, so the house is ≈15.5 m square — consistent with the 49-foot figure (in the larger Danish foot) recorded above. The foundation stone was laid on 8 August 1576 by Charles de Dançay (b. 1509 near Poitiers, d. 1589), the French envoy, in the south-east corner, 'when the rising Sun together with Jupiter was in the heart of Leo, while the Moon was in the western heavens in Aquarius'; a fragment of the porphyry stone found early in the twentieth century in the wall of a house on the island reads FOELICIS AVSPICII ERGO / ANNO MDLXXVI / KA.AVGVSTI P. — the first of August, not the eighth: the 1996 editors suggest the date was cut on the stone for 1 August and the ceremony then put back a week to catch the favourable aspect (Hadravová, Hadrava & Shackelford 1996, pp. 147–148, nn. 260–262). The same edition notes (n. 258) that a thorough archaeological exploration of Hven begun in 1988 was providing what a reconstruction of the buildings and of Tycho's gardens needed, and that the first part of the garden was opened in 1992.
Dimensions. central building a square of 49 feet per side; round towers 18 feet in diameter on the north and south sides; narrow entrance towers east and west (Dreyer, p. 96, from Tycho's own Epistolae astronomicae p. 218 + Mechanica fol. H.4)
Fate. decay documented from Tycho's departure — royal commissioners Thomas Fincke and Iver Stub were sent on 2 June 1597 to inspect the damage the Hven tenants had done to the instruments still on Uraniborg (KB 1596–1602 p. 163); by June 1602 the buildings were reported 'meget forfalden' and surveyed for the new lensmand Cort Barleben (KB p. 746). Demolished and quarried in the 17th century; only foundations survive, partly restored

Excavation. C. V. L. Charlier excavated the foundations in 1901. The recovered ground-plan does not agree with the plans Tycho himself printed (which are wrong “end ikke i de store Hovedtræk” — not even in the main outlines); the reconstruction therefore follows the excavated measurements, not the printed figures. What Charlier actually recorded (1901, pp. 5, 7–9): the east and west foundation walls of the square well preserved, standing half a metre and in places more than a metre above the basement floor, the square measuring 1430 cm east–west and 1445 cm along the east wall — against Tycho's 60 pedes a foot of 23.8 cm (d'Arrest had 25.4 cm, Picard 31.6); the south wing's outer wall as a segment of a circle 910 cm north–south by 1300 cm east–west, with no trace of the inner circular wall; in the north wing part of the inner wall, cylindrical, 6 m across, with Tycho's well at its centre, still in use; the south-east cellar cleared by Ekdahl in 1823 (280 by 285 cm, its floor 2 m below the basement), which he was inclined to take for the castle prison; and no trace of the fountain. The plans were drawn by Landskrona's city architect Fredrik Sundbärg and E. F. Larsson (p. 5). Of the library rotunda itself, then, the ground preserved nothing but the outer ambulatory wall.

Design source. The design adapts Palladio's Villa Rotonda (known via the engraving in I quattro libri dell'architettura, 1570): Uraniborg keeps the square plan and the four axial corridors but drops the central circular domed hall (impractical in a Nordic house), substitutes octagonal portal-projections for the open loggias, and adds the two round towers N and S. Vitruvian symmetria — invoked by Tycho in the Mechanica's description of the east front, after the architectural learning he had professed since his 1574 oration — governs the whole; the volute window-crowns follow Serlio, the timber cupola Walter Rivius's German Vitruvius (1548). [Jern 1976 later relocates the façade source from the Veneto villa to Venetian church fronts — a competing attribution.]

The gardens and ramparts of Uraniborg

reconstructedB1576; redesigned after 1590 · Ven (Hven)site-gardens
The square earthwork ramparts enclosing Uraniborg, their corners set to the cardinal points, with formal gardens and orchards within and around them — among the finest in Scandinavia by Tycho's own account. Christianson describes the orchard's pippin, Rambour and Gylling apples, Doyenné and blood pears, green and blue Reine Claude plums, morello cherries, apricots, figs, quince and walnuts, and knot gardens laid out as a microcosm; Tycho's sister Sophie Brahe is recorded helping lay out and replant the garden (boxwood cuttings noted in his diary). Tycho rebuilt the ramparts and redesigned and replanted the knot gardens and orchards in the renovations of c.1591. Beyond its beauty the garden carried a symbolic-alchemical programme (Michelsen, in Tycho Brahes Verden, 2006): the medicinal plants were set in precisely planned beds so as to absorb the maximum heavenly influence (a Paracelsian-medicine principle), the enclosed garden read as a microcosm of the cosmic order and a figure of the Opus Magnum — one of the main elements of Tycho's 'earthly chemistry', in harmony with the astronomy pursued in Urania's house at its centre.
Fate. lost; the geometry recovered by excavation and partly recreated on site

The alchemical laboratory

lostB1580 · Ven (Hven)site-laboratory
The alchemical laboratory in Uraniborg's basement, fitted with sixteen furnaces of nine different kinds — Tycho's 'terrestrial astronomy', where he prepared medicines (the plague elixirs) and pursued the Paracelsian correspondence of macrocosm and microcosm. Visitors were taken down to see it; at the basement's other end lay cellars, pantries and the deep well. Tycho later added a second, smaller laboratory of five furnaces in the converted winter dining room (Thoren, Lord of Uraniborg, pp. 159, 224).
Fate. lost with Uraniborg; no trace of the furnaces survives

The library and the great globe

lostA1580 · Ven (Hven)site-library
Uraniborg's library was the Museum: the round room on the ground floor of the south tower, with the chemical laboratory in the crypt beneath it and the great southern observatory above. Tycho's printed Synopsis of 1596 places it, in the third person, ‘in media contignatione rotundæ Turriculæ versus Austrum’ — on the middle storey of the round turret towards the south — ‘where Tycho's students apply themselves to their studies by day and carry out astronomical operations, and where his library is also found’ (Epistolae 1596, pp. 237–240 = Opera omnia VI, 268); the German description that went to Landgrave Wilhelm IV with the letter of 26 September 1591, compiled by Tycho's German amanuensis as the letter's postscript says, has the same (Opera omnia VI, 255). On every plan Tycho printed — the Marburg sheet of 1586, the Epistolae of 1596, the Mechanica of 1598 — the letters T Bibliotheca and W Globus magnus orichalcicus stand in the south rotunda of the ground floor, and the elevation key reads ‘M. Musæum cum Bibliotheca’ (in 1586 ‘Musæum rotundum cum Bibliotheca et Globo magno aurichalcico, cuius Diameter 6 pedum est’). Braun & Hogenberg's Civitates orbis terrarum IV — the Hven plate ‘Effigiata Coloniae 1586’, published 1588 — reprints that 1586 key in Latin and, in the French edition, as ‘L'estude rond, avec une librairie & un grand Globus ou boulle faicte de laiton’: a derivative witness to the 1586 state, aviaries included. A column stood in the middle of the room: describing the painting behind the mural quadrant, Tycho sets the great globe ‘post columnam, in medio Musæi rotundi constitutam’ (Mechanica, Opera omnia V, 31), and the engraving of 1598 shows the column rising through laboratory, Museum and observatory. Round it stood four tables at which he kept six, eight, at times twelve students computing, besides boys and juniors (plan key ‘V. Quatuor Mensæ pro Studiosis’); six or eight other globes, celestial and terrestrial, smaller instruments and automata; on the wall between the windows the painted boards of Hipparchus, Ptolemy, Albategnius and Copernicus, of Landgrave Wilhelm, of George Buchanan (Peter Young's gift) and of seven Italian philosophers on one board; a porphyry stone with Plato's dictum in gold letters, ‘Unum est naturæ vinculum, doctrina numerorum figurarum et motuum cœlestium’, and the inscription ‘Non haberi, sed esse’ (Opera omnia VI, 368). The books appear once in a contemporary image: the 1598 engraving shows, at the letter Tycho explains as ‘a part of our library’, two open bracket shelves with the volumes standing upright. The room was open to the students — Christian Hansen Riber deposed in 1598 that they ‘examined your whole library whenever we pleased’ (Jardine & Segonds 2008, p. 169) — and in September 1584 Ursus lodged in it and handled the papers on its tables, where a copy of the prosthaphaeresis rules lay for each student (Tycho to Hagecius, 14 March 1592, Opera omnia VII, 321–326). Of the room itself nothing was found in the ground: Charlier's excavation of 1901 recovered the foundations of the south wing's outer wall — a segment of a circle, 910 cm north–south by 1300 cm east–west — and records that of the inner circular wall ‘nu intet spår finnes kvar’, no trace now remains (Charlier 1901, p. 7); no pillar base, floor or wall thickness was seen. The inner diameter of ‘a little over 5 m’ in the modern literature (Jern 1976, pp. 38–39; Mortensen 1955, p. 31) is therefore read off the reconstructed plan, not off an excavated wall; Tycho prints 20 feet (1586) and 22 feet (1596, 1598) for the towers, and Charlier's own foot, derived from the 1430 cm square against Tycho's 60 pedes, is 23.8 cm (p. 9). The two aviaries of the 1586 key — ‘R. Cavea aviaria versus Occidentem. Q. Alia cavea aviaria versus ortum, in quibus aves liberè in parvis arboribus hinc inde volantes cantitant’ — closed the ends of the ambulatory on either side of the room; the keys of 1596 and 1598 omit them. Copernicus's triquetrum was not kept here but in the great northern observatory (plan key R). The one description of the interior by a visitor independent of Tycho's texts is Augustin von Mörsberg's, from his visit of 3 July 1592 (printed by Zeiller, 1648): a hall ‘upstairs’ painted with the most famous astronomers, Landgrave Wilhelm among them, from which one enters ‘a fine round tower’ holding ‘a very great celestial globe’; next to the hall Tycho's bedchamber with the mural quadrant. Georg Ludwig Frobenius, taken on in May 1591 — ‘as a student of astronomy in his observatory’ in the Danish translation of his memoir, ‘in his Museum’ in Christianson's — describes neither the room nor the globe nor the books (KB NKS 2596 fol., tr. Butler & Christianson 1989). No one counted the books in Tycho's lifetime; the size of the library is treated in the Bibliotheca chapter. Albrecht's survey of 13 August 1901 (Das Weltall 2, 1901–02, pp. 10 and 275, with the plan ‘Die Reste der Uranienburg’ at 1:160) found the north tower's inner ring standing — ‘nur der innere erhalten, dessen Radius von ca. 3 m mit dem auf Tycho's Grundriss verzeichneten gut übereinstimmt’ — and on the south side only the outer ring and two bricked holes; on the plan the ring measures 5.95 m over the wall and about 4.8 m inside, and Tycho's 20 feet (1586) and 22 feet (1596) for the towers agree with the excavated square and the found rings as outer measures, so that the library room, which the key of 1586 makes the north tower's equal, was about 4.8–5.1 m across inside.
Fate. lost with Uraniborg

Stjerneborg — the sunken observatory (1584)

reconstructedA1584 · Ven (Hven)site-stjerneborg
A separate, largely subterranean observatory built in 1584 just south of Uraniborg: five crypts beneath rotating cupolas, each giving one of the largest instruments a permanent, wind-sheltered mounting on solid ground. Stjerneborg ('star castle') answered the chief defect of Uraniborg's high galleries — that wind and an unstable footing spoiled the finest measurements. In his own words, Tycho built it “about the year 1584, on a hill outside the castle … with no small difficulty and expenditure a subterranean observatory with various cellars … of solid masonry,” his purpose “partly to have placed some of the most important instruments securely and firmly in order that they should not be exposed to the disturbing influence of the wind, and should be easier to use,” and partly to separate simultaneous observers so “they should not get in the way of each other” (Mechanica, trans. Ræder, Strömgren & Strömgren 1946, pp. 135–137).
Dimensions. outer premises 70 feet per side; the central semicircles 24 feet in diameter — Tycho's own figures (Mechanica 1946, p. 137; in his 259 mm foot ≈ 18 m and ≈ 6.2 m)
Fate. ruined after 1597; the crypts excavated and reconstructed in the 20th century — now the Tycho Brahe Museum

The printing house (Tycho's press)

lostA1584 · Ven (Hven)site-printing-house
Tycho's own printing house, established on Hven in 1584 so that he controlled his own means of publication and distribution. The first thing printed was the poem to Erik Lange, on 27 November 1584, by Tycho's own protocol entry in the 1584 observation volume — 'omnium primo Carmen ad Ericum Lange' in his hand (Opera omnia X, 302; Friis 1868–69, p. 253) — of which no copy survives; the memorial broadsheet Epitaphium Johannis Pratensis (1584) is the earliest print of which a copy survives, and the occasional poem Carmen ad Jacobum Ulfeldium, inscribed 'Uraniburgi, 7 Kal. Decemb.' = 25 November 1584, carries the earliest printed date (Nielsen 1921, pp. 178–179). The first true book was Elias Olsen Morsing's Diarium astrologicum et meteorologicum / Calendarium (calendar set late 1585, issued 1586). The press went on to produce De mundi (1588), the Danish-language Flemløs Astrologia (1591), the Epistolae (1596) and the advance sheets of the Progymnasmata (1592) — a work only completed at Prague in 1602.
Fate. lost; the press and types dispersed after Tycho left Hven

The paper mill

lostB1589–1597 · Ven (Hven)site-paper-mill
The paper mill on Hven that fed Tycho's press — its production a recurring concern in the correspondence (the two papermakers who absconded delayed the Progymnasmata, as Tycho complained to Hájek). Royal chancery records fix the mill's economics and fate: a privilege of 2 March 1592 ordered all inhabitants of Nørrejylland to sell their old linen rags (Lumper) to Tycho before anyone else, since he 'har anlagt en Papirmølle paa Hveen' and needed them to make paper (KB 1588–92 p. 750). A demolition order of 26 June 1602 to the new lensmand Cort Barleben locates the works 'syd for Uraniborg' and calls it a 'Stampemølle eller Papirmølle,' by then wholly derelict, to be torn down and its usable timber and stone re-used (KB 1596–1602 p. 751). The mill dam was built by Valentin von Spangenberg (also written 'Valenthin'), a hydraulic engineer previously employed by Frederick II at Kronborg (1578–88), who arrived on Hven in 1589. The Meteorological Diary records on 26 June 1589 that Valentin promised to complete the mølledæmning (mill dam) within four weeks, and on 22 July 1590: 'Abijt Valentinus opere aggeris apud molendinum confecto' — Valentin departed after completing the dam work (Vellev 2017, from TBOO IX). Tycho named the paper mill's purpose explicitly in a letter to Christoph Rothmann of 24 November 1589 (TBOO VI); Christian IV ordered oak timber felled for the stamp press in a letter to Kirsten Lindenov (Steen Bille's widow, Tycho's aunt) dated 19 March 1590 (Kancelliets Brevbøger 1588–1592, 360). Its ruined site was rediscovered by the amateur astronomer N. A. Møller Nicolaisen (1874–1954), who on a 1929 visit to Hven noticed a man-made mound and identified it as the fallen paper mill; the Scandinavian Tycho Brahe Foundation funded his excavations of 1933–34, an archæological sensation of the period (Vellev 2002). Vellev's account of the excavation (2002) fills in the site. Tycho wrote to a correspondent in Germany in 1589 that the mill was already finished — a high, wide embankment regulating the water, a wheel of about seven metres driven by the least possible water and powering two other works besides the paper-making, fishponds laid out to feed it — but the monument he set on the embankment dates the work 'begun 1590, completed 1592', and Vellev reads the letter as ahead of the fact. The stone, moved to Knutstorp probably in 1740 and still on the outer wall of the main building there, is split from top to bottom with its back half lost, the inscription badly worn; it was re-examined in 1996. Earlier writers had put the mill at the stream's present outfall into the Sound; Møller Nicolaisen placed it about eighty metres further north, at the five-metre contour below the dam whose crest reaches thirteen metres (surveyed by G. Wilske, 1932–33). Digging began on 24 July 1933 with Torsten Mårtensson of the Helsingborg museum attached for the measurements: roof tiles and glazed flagstones on the first day, vertical posts and fieldstones a couple of metres down, then a stone-built wheel-race full of mud, and on 1 August, at its bottom, a 2.5-metre fragment of the wheel — an oak rim 26 cm wide with its grooves cut out, bucket-boards of pine, the diameter computed at 6.94 metres; conserved at the National Museum in Copenhagen, it is shown in the museum by Uraniborg. A shaft four metres down from the dam's crest found the wood-and-iron launder that had carried water from the last pond over the top of the wheel. The Swedish crown prince and princess were shown the site on 22 July 1934; when the second season closed the cleared area was covered over and marked with four concrete posts. The finds are divided between the museum on Hven and the Museum of Cultural History in Lund, the Swedish participants' negatives between Helsingborg and Stockholm; Møller Nicolaisen's reconstructions of the ground plan (water wheel, rag beater, hammer mill for hides, grindstone, parchment room, forming room, a room of Tycho's own) and of the north-west front are, in Vellev's words, attempts of which almost every solution can be questioned. He published in Nordisk Astronomisk Tidsskrift (1930–38) and in Tycho Brahes Papirmølle paa Hven (Gyldendal, 1946); his archive — measurements, photographs, correspondence and the slide series of his lectures — passed to Vellev in 1996. Of the mill's paper three watermarks are known: Uraniborg with the legend VRANIBVRGVM, and two with the Brahe arms, the first two on book paper and the third on writing paper. Nielsen's dated bounds for the mill: by 25 January 1590 Tycho had engaged a papermaker 'til sin nye Papirmølle' and sent him to Lübeck, where he went missing, and by 3 August 1590 both papermakers were gone (Nielsen 1921, p. 191); the preface to Flemløs's Astrologia of June 1591 attests the mill at work — 'hand lader nu paa sin Papyr Mølle … berede Papyr vdi forraad til samme Opera' (p. 192). Nielsen knew the mill's paper by two watermarks; Vellev counts three. An object to check: Paulli and Dal (1959, p. 13) record in Harald Mortensen's collection at the Royal Library 'et dejligt stykke gammel eg, som har været hjulskovl i Uraniborgs papirmølle', excavated in 1933 and given by forvalter Gösta Alm on Hven — a paddle-wheel blade distinct from, or part of, the wheel fragment conserved at the National Museum. Two Danish museum records belong to this history. Roskilde Museum holds a brick 'fra Tycho Brahes papirmølle på Hveen (1589), fundet her 1933' (ROMU 1935-0124) — a find of the first season that left the Hven–Lund division of the finds. And the discovery itself was reported in Berlingske Tidende on 1 August 1929 under the heading 'Et Tycho Brahe minde': Harald Mortensen told how he and the amateur astronomer Nicolaisen of Vejle had found the remains of the paper mill, the observations made possible by Nicolaisen's knowledge of water mills (Museum Odense DMM/00062x036740, the cutting) — the report names Mortensen beside Nicolaisen, where Vellev's account credits Nicolaisen's 1929 visit alone.
Fate. lost; derelict by 1602 and demolished for salvage on royal order (26 June 1602, to lensmand Cort Barleben; KB 1596–1602 p. 751); site rediscovered and excavated 1933–34

Bohemia — the exile 6 sites

The Belvedere (Royal Summer Palace) — Tycho's Prague Musaeum

extantB1599–1635 · Praguesite-belvedere
The Renaissance Royal Summer Palace (Belvedere) in the Royal Garden below Prague Castle. Tycho is reported to have made Prague observations 'in the arcades of the building' (Pierre Bergeron, 1603); after his death in 1601 the Belvedere became a memorial 'Astronomical Museum' housing the astronomy and exact-science part of Rudolf II's collections — the large instruments together with portraits of astronomers (Ptolemy, Albategnius, Copernicus, and two of Tycho himself) and of their royal patrons (Alfonso X, Charles V, Rudolf II, Frederik II of Denmark). The two surviving Tycho-associated sextants by Bürgi and Habermel passed through this collection on their way to the National Technical Museum. Dreyer (1890, p. 365) corroborates the connection from the other side: the Emperor agreed to buy Tycho's own Hven instruments from the family for 20,000 thaler; Tengnagel, as scientific heir from 1602, denied Kepler access to them and had them vaulted under Curtius' house, and Kepler later recorded the 'instrumenta Braheana' as standing 'in viridario Caesaris' (the Emperor's garden, i.e. this Belvedere) in October 1604 — before they were destroyed in the 1619 Bohemian rising against the Habsburgs. Not to be confused with Tycho's principal Prague-period working sites, Benátky nad Jizerou and the Curtius house — the latter the very building under which the instruments were stored. Grade B: the 'Musaeum' reading is Fučíková's, and Tycho's own use of the Belvedere is reported, not firmly documented. Danish painters of the nineteenth century knew the building as 'Tycho Brahes observatorium': Thorald Læssøe's two views carry that title in the Statens Museum for Kunst's records (KMS418, dated 1843, and KMS4365, whose catalogue date of 1831 predates the painter's birth in 1816 and must be a slip).
Fate. the building survives; its astronomical contents dispersed after 1612 and removed to the Gallery Building by 1635

Benátky nad Jizerou — Tycho's first Bohemian observatory

extantB1599–1600 · Benátky nad Jizerousite-benatky
The hilltop castle at Benátky nad Jizerou, about 30 km north of Prague, granted to Tycho by Rudolf II in 1599 as his first Bohemian observatory. He was given leave to build wooden observing cubicles (now vanished); the portion available to him ran to three floors of nearly 500 m² each. Here Johannes Kepler joined Tycho in February 1600, before Tycho moved to Prague proper (Thoren, Appendix 5).
Fate. the hilltop castle survives (renovated, intended to house a small Tycho museum); the wooden observing cubicles are gone

The Golden Griffin inn (U Zlatého noha), Nový Svět — Tycho's first Prague lodging of 1600

extantB1600 · Praguesite-golden-griffin
The inn 'at the Golden Griffin' on Nový Svět, the lane below the castle on the north side of Hradčany, where Tycho and his household were put up when the Emperor's summons brought them from Benátky in June 1600 — 'Eram adhuc in publico hospitio apud Insigne aurei Gryphei', he wrote to Kepler in August 1600 (Opera omnia VIII, 341), before he rented a house near Baron Hoffmann's. The identification with the present house, cadastral number 76/IV, which carries a plaque of 1901, was doubted by Jansa and Herain in 1902; Kølln gives the building as two storeys of 15 × 9 m (Kølln 1995, pp. 67, 69). Norlind dates the move to about the beginning of July 1600, the modern biographies to June; the lodging lasted weeks, not months. Grade B: the lodging is Tycho's own statement, the house's identification a local tradition questioned in print.
Fate. the house stands — the present two-storey building of 15 × 9 m on Nový Svět, cadastral number 76/IV, with a memorial plaque of 1901

The Curtius (Kurtz) house — Tycho's Prague residence and place of death

lostB1600–1601 · Praguesite-curtius-house
The summer home built by the imperial vice-chancellor Jacob Kurtz (Curtius), a few minutes' walk west of the Imperial Castle on the Hradčany hill — Tycho's principal Prague residence for most of his Bohemian years (the 20,000-gulden edifice he described) and the house in which he died on 24 October 1601. After his death the imperial-purchased instruments were vaulted under this very house before passing to the Belvedere garden (Dreyer, p. 365; cf. site-belvedere). Only a 7×16 m foundation remnant survives by a later school (Thoren, Appendix 5). Kurtz did not live to see the letter he had written to Tycho printed in the Mechanica: he died in 1594 at the age of forty, 'as can be read on his gravestone in the St. Thomas' church in the Lesser Town of Prague', and the house he had built on Pohořelec, to which he had once invited Tycho, was bought from his widow by the Emperor — the house in which Tycho and later Kepler found their common lodging (Hadravová, Hadrava & Shackelford 1996, Prologue p. viii). Mortensen, visiting in the 1930s, gives the excavated ground plan as 'cir. 17 M. i □', describes from the excavation reports walls painted in fresco, door frames of red marble and sandstone windows with gilded palmettes, and notes the tower still on David's survey of 1804 (Mortensen 1942, pp. 17–18); Kølln adds the excavations of 1902 and 1931 under R. Hlubinka's report, the plaque at the school entrance, Antonín Lhoták's bust inside, and the 1971 competition whose third-prize winner, Josef Vajce, made the statue placed in 1983 (Kølln 1995, p. 67). The house stood, as the Strahov curator Jan Pařez notes, in the immediate neighbourhood of the Strahov monastery and within its separate jurisdiction (postranní právo) at Pohořelec — so that the five volumes from Tycho's library now in the Strahov library are kept a few hundred metres from where he died (Pařez 2009, p. 26). The library followed the household into the house: Šolc places it there — ‘Später kam seine Bibliothek nach Prag in das Curtius-Haus’ (1999, § 2.531, printed p. 150; 2006, p. 192, ‘do Kurzova domu dorazily konečně i knihy’) — giving no date and no order; the order is the bibliotheca's chronology: the books, held at Magdeburg since 1599, reached Prague in mid-November 1600 (Norlind 1970, p. 334), the familia moved in on 25 February 1601, and Thoren has the library ‘moved into the Kurtz house’ (bibliotheca tycho-brahe-library, the exile section). Šolc's list of the pupils and co-workers who lived in the house includes Willem Blaeu, whom the familia places on Hven in 1595–96 only; that name is not adopted here. The National Library's volume of 2026 dates the unveiling of Vajce's group to 1984 and names the school on the site, the Gymnázium Jana Keplera; Vackářová writes that the Kurz house was demolished when Prague's Baroque fortifications were built (printed p. 30 (scan 30), Obr. 40 and its caption) — a statement recorded as hers, since Mortensen has the tower still standing on David's survey of 1804 (1942, pp. 17–18) and the two are not reconciled.
Fate. demolished — the observation tower was still standing in 1804, when Alois David measured it (Trigonometrische Vermessungen, Prague 1805); the foundations were excavated in 1902 and again in 1930–31 when the school was built, with an official report and measurements by R. Hlubinka (Zprávy Památkového Sboru hlav. města Prahy, 1931, p. 131); a 7×16 m remnant is fenced in the park before the school; a plinth was unveiled in 1971 after that year's statue competition; Josef Vajce's group of Tycho and Kepler was placed on it in 1983 (Kølln 1995, p. 67) and unveiled in 1984 (Vackářová 2026, printed p. 30 (scan 30), Obr. 40) — placing and unveiling: the Czech monument databases hrady.cz and drobnepamatky.cz (entry of 25 May 2018) likewise give 1984 as the year the group was unveiled before the Gymnázium Jana Keplera, so Kølln's 1983 reads as the placing; the record's earlier uncited 1984 now has two witnesses

The Schwarzenberg (Rožmberk) palace — Tycho's last banquet

extantC1601 · Praguesite-schwarzenberg
The Renaissance palace on Hradčany square, then owned by Petr Vok z Rožmberka (who had acquired it from Rudolf II by exchange in 1600) — by Thoren's reckoning (Appendix 5) the site of the banquet of 13 October 1601 at which Tycho fell fatally ill. The contemporary story holds that, out of deference to his host Petr Vok, he held his urine too long at table; he sickened with a bladder/uraemic ailment and died eleven days later, on 24 October 1601, at the Curtius house. Grade C: the banquet's location at the Rožmberk palace follows Thoren; the death-by-retention tale is contemporary tradition, not established fact (the 2010 exhumation only excluded mercury poisoning — see the Receptio record of the 2010 exhumation).
Fate. the palace survives on Hradčany square (now a museum)

Church of Our Lady before Týn — Tycho's grave

extantA1601 · Praguesite-tyn-church
The Church of Our Lady before Týn on Prague's Old Town Square, where Tycho was buried on 4 November 1601. His tomb is marked by a relief effigy in armour with one hand on a celestial globe. The grave's afterlife — the 1901 and 2010 exhumations and the long Danish–Czech contest of memory — is modelled in the Reception pillar. What lies under the paving — the brick vault over an oblong chamber beneath the marble gravestone, the two burials of 1901's finding, the relaying of the floor in 1721, the refurbished vault of 29 July 1901 — is recorded from Matiegka's report of 1901 in the Reception pillar's grave record.
