1Introduction

This is a register of 53 episodes in the reception of Tycho Brahe. An episode is one documented instance of his work or his person being received, used, edited, contested, commemorated or examined: a transfer of data, a book built on his observations, a tomb opened, a crater named, a poem solicited, a claim on a treasury. The register is deliberately not confined to the years after his death. Reception began in his lifetime — the vernacular reading of the 1572 star, the liminary verse he solicited for his own books, the charter for Uraniborg’s perpetuity, a Danish nobleman’s hostile verdict of 1598 — and 13 of the 53 episodes begin before 1601. The other 40 run from the inheritance of the observations in October 1601 to analyses published in this decade; 4 of them have no end, because the thing they concern still exists.

Every episode carries a stable key, a thread, an evidence grade, the works it descends from, the sources it rests on, and, where one could be verified, a link to the object itself or to a digitised copy of the edition.

SN 1572 — Tycho's Supernova and its remnant

1572 / todaySkyBextantrecep-nova-remnant

The new star of 1572 that Tycho recorded in De nova stella is now known as SN 1572 ('Tycho's Supernova'), a Type Ia whose expanding remnant (3C 10) is studied today in X-rays and through light echoes.

The 1572 new star in contemporary eyes — Busch & Reravius (1572–73)

1572–1573ScienceArecep-busch-reravius-1572

Tycho's De nova stella (1573) was only one of many contemporary responses to the new star — and, in Denmark, not the one that reached the public first. The German Lutheran preacher Georg Busch read the 1572 phenomenon as a comet, advancing an unusual Christian comet-theory in which it was not merely a warning from God but was physically condensed out of 'evaporated' earthly sin (a notion found only in the Lutheran north). The Danish priest Rasmus Hansen Reravius translated Busch into Danish (Copenhagen, in at least two editions; it also seeded a Swedish version), drawn to the book for its message of a warning sent by God to a sinful humanity — the comet-or-star question being, to him, secondary. Reravius enlarged Busch's title from the German 'On the comet' to the Danish 'On the new star and comet', adding the word 'star' under the influence of Tycho, whose Latin treatise had by then established that it was a star, not a comet. Reravius's vernacular book reached far more readers than Tycho's small-run, slowly-distributed Latin treatise, and so became the channel through which Tycho's discovery first reached a broad public (Fink-Jensen).

The apocalyptic sky — the 1572 nova and 1577 comet read as signs

1572–1592SkyBrecep-apocalyptic-nova

To Tycho and many of his contemporaries the 1572 new star and the 1577 comet were not first of all cosmological discoveries but divine signs. His German report on the comet — the language Queen Sophie read — dwells far more on its calamitous astrological meaning than on the finding that comets lie beyond the Moon; and when he finished the Progymnasmata he closed it (1592) with what Håkansson calls a prophetic testament: the nova as an omen of religious upheaval, the seven great conjunctions since Creation mapped onto the biblical epochs, the conjunction of 1583 (the seventh) inaugurating a sabbath-like golden age. On this reading Tycho looked at the heavens 'with the eyes of another age' — not natural phenomena dissolving the medieval cosmos, but God's punishing hand driving history to its fulfilment. An interpretive thread (Håkansson 2006), who cautions explicitly against the Whig reading of the nova as 'the decisive step to modern cosmology'. The prophetic reading was not the only meaning the new star carried in Tycho's own pages: in the Elegy to Urania in De nova stella (1573) the muse's epiphany gives Tycho the nova as a personal calling, setting the heavenly worth of science against the emptiness of conventional noble ideals — the beginning of the Urania allegory that Zeeberg traces from the humble alumnus of 1573, through the 1584–85 Uraniborg poems in which Uraniborg is astronomy's sole refuge, to Tycho's late, complete identification of himself with astronomy (Zeeberg 1993).

Tycho in contemporary verse — æredigte and support-poems (1573–1602)

1573–1602BiographyArecep-aeredigte-reception

The earliest printed reception of Tycho was in verse. De nova stella (1573) opens and closes with liminary poems (æredigte) by Hans Frandsen and Anders Sørensen Vedel — guarantees from university and church for a young nobleman publishing in the academic manner. Tycho came to manage the genre deliberately. For the Progymnasmata he ran a solicitation campaign through his correspondence (1590–92): to Camerarius the younger in Nürnberg he sent his engraved portrait with a request for a portrait-poem from Paulus Melissus, and he approached Oxford, Görlitz, Prague, Edinburgh and Vienna in the same terms; the poem Martinus Mylius supplied for the provisional 1592 edition Tycho edited himself so that, as he told Scultetus, it should not seem too arrogant; and King James VI of Scotland contributed two autograph epigrams, printed in the posthumous Prague edition of 1602. The Epistolae astronomicae (1596) carry six æredigte, and the Mechanica (1598) its own set — among them Holger Rosenkrantz's poem, which Tycho himself lengthened to fill the page. In the departure years 1597–1600 the current reversed: collections of support-poems from sympathisers followed Tycho into exile, surviving in Vienna and Copenhagen transcripts — probably excerpts of one larger gathering. Zeeberg reads the whole practice as deliberate image-management: to an unusual degree the contemporary poetic reception of Tycho was solicited, edited and staged by Tycho himself.

Tycho against Ursus — the quarrel over the geo-heliocentric system and its historiography (1584–1601; 1890–2008)

1584–1601ScienceArecep-ursus-quarrel

The quarrel began with Erik Lange’s visit to Hven in September 1584, when his attendant Nicolaus Reimers Ursus took or copied, by Tycho’s account, a sketch of the system Tycho published in the De mundi of 1588; Ursus printed the system as his own in the Fundamentum astronomicum (Strasbourg 1588) and answered Tycho’s charge of plagiarism in De astronomicis hypothesibus (Prague 1597) with insults that Tycho had marked in a copy lent to Holger Rosenkrantz and never recovered. Tycho gathered depositions — Christian Hansen Riber’s at Leiden and Michael Walther’s at Kassel in May 1598, Lange’s at Magdeburg in October — sued at Prague through the advocate Johannes Fritsch, had Johannes Müller draw up a formal refutation of Ursus’s Demonstratio on 23 August 1600, and set Kepler to the Apologia pro Tychone contra Ursum; Ursus died on 15 August 1600 before judgement; with the archbishop of Prague’s collaboration the copies found at his house were burned, and the Hofkammer compensated his widow on 23 October 1600 (Jardine & Segonds 2008, pp. 178, 190). The papers went with Kepler’s to St Petersburg. Dreyer (1890) left the plagiarism ‘not proved’; Jardine edited and analysed the Contra Ursum (1984, 1988); Launert wrote Ursus’s life (1999); Jardine and Segonds edited the dossier of letters and depositions with the Contra Ursum in La guerre des astronomes (2008), whose introduction judges Ursus’s claim of priority a bluff, the question of plagiarism probably insoluble, Ursus’s ‘furtum philosophicum’ a concession rather than a confession, and Tycho cleared of having falsified Rothmann’s letters in the Epistolae of 1596 — verdicts recorded here as theirs (2008, pp. 8–11, 27 n. 1, 107–118, 146–156, 171–176, 188–196, 242–243). Gingerich and Westman's monograph of 1988 is the fullest narrative in English: Tycho's three versions of the theft — to Brucaeus on 4 November 1588 (Opera omnia VII, p. 149), to Rantzau on 21 December 1588 (VII, pp. 387–389: the drawing on the table for Lange, the servant sent out of the room, the discarded sheet on a window sill with Mars's orb enclosing the sun's) and to Rothmann on 21 February 1589 (VI, p. 179) — and the ‘legal brief’ to Hagecius of 14 March 1592 (VII, pp. 321–326), translated nearly whole (pp. 52–62), with which Tycho sent Hagecius the discarded sheet itself, now lost; Ursus's dedicatory epistle of 31 July 1588 as the earliest possible date of the Fundamentum (n. 143); Dudith's enquiry of 30 October 1588 and his letter of 25 January 1589 with a diagram of Wittich's system that Wittich ‘boasted that he communicated … to Tycho’ (pp. 49–50, Fig. 8); Gellius Sascerides confronting Ursus at Strasbourg; Ursus's side note ‘Let it be a theft, but a philosophical one’ (sig. F, Fig. 13), read with Jardine as the rhetoric of confessio; Kepler's reading of May 1599; and the authors' chronology of 1584–1587, which leaves open whether Ursus and Wittich met at Kassel in the autumn of 1585 (pp. 67–69). They date Tycho's settled conviction about the interpenetration of the orbs to January 1587 rather than to the 1583 he later claimed (p. 75).

The endowment that failed — Uraniborg's promised perpetuity (1588) against the instruments' decay (1597)

1588–1597LegacyBrecep-uraniborg-endowment

Uraniborg was meant to outlast its founder. On 23 August 1588, in the regency after Frederik II's death, the Council of the Realm issued at Tycho's request an open charter undertaking that the building he had raised on Hven 'med maerkelig Bekostning' and its instruments be kept at Crown expense in the state they were then in, during and after his lifetime, endowed from canonries and other benefices, with his own kin — failing them native Danish nobility, failing them other Danes — preferred to succeed him in the work; the full Council of the Realm re-issued it verbatim on 17 July 1589 (Kancelliets Brevbøger 1588–92, pp. 82–83, 213). It was the formal charter of Uraniborg as a perpetual institution — and it was not kept. Within a decade Tycho had lost the fief, and on 2 June 1597 royal commissioners Thomas Fincke and Iver Stub were sent to Hven to inspect the damage the island's tenants had done to the astronomical instruments still standing on Uraniborg (KB 1596–1602, p. 163) — the first documented harm to the great instruments, more than twenty years before their final destruction in the Bohemian upheaval of 1619.

Dudith reads De mundi — the first reader's response to the Tychonic system, in red ink in the margins of his presentation copy (Stockholm, RAR 106 A), 1588–1589

1588–1589ScienceBextantrecep-dudith-de-mundi-1588

Andreas Dudith (1533–1589), the Hungarian humanist and former bishop living out his last years among his books at Breslau, was one of the first readers Tycho chose for the De mundi: a copy went to him in May 1588 in the full Uraniborg livery — white parchment, the gilt portrait oval, green silk ties — with an autograph dedication dated 1 May 1588, and on 17 August Tycho asked both Hagecius and Scultetus to greet him and, above all, to obtain his judgement of the book in writing before winter closed the roads. Dudith read it with a pen in his hand, in the red ink he preferred: an index on the front flyleaf to the two pages that mattered to him — p. 188, where Tycho promises the ‘Opus de Astronomiae redintegratione’, and p. 189, the ‘Nova mundani systematis hypotyposis’ — and marginalia on some thirty openings between p. 13 and p. 287, after which the pen stops. He keyworded the argument (‘fixar. loca non rite obseruata’ at the chapter on the fixed stars; ‘Laus Moestlini eximia’ at Tycho's praise of Maestlin; ‘astrol.’ at the disclaimer of astrology), worked the spherical triangles beside the diagrams of pp. 16–65 as a mathematician checks a proof, and reduced the eighth chapter to four strokes: ‘hoc opus, hic labor est’ where Tycho states the problem of a hypothesis satisfying mathematics, physics and theology at once; ‘terra immota’; ‘reliq. pl[ane]tae 5 circa ☉ gyran.’; and under the famous diagram, ‘Terra centrum octauae sphaerae, ☉ et ☽; reliq. 5 pl[ane]tae circa ☉ gyrantur’, with a red ♀ added to Venus's orb. No written judgement reached Hven: Hagecius wrote on 10 May 1589 that Dudith had died about two months before (23 February), and Tycho mourned on 1 November 1589 the man he ‘had resolved to contract friendship with by letters’ and to send a student with an instrument. The copy went with the rest of Dudith's library to Cardinal Dietrichstein at Nikolsburg, in Swedish barrels to Stockholm after Torstensson took the town in 1645, and stayed in the royal library (Dudith's accession number 61 on the head edge; Christina's red T on the spine) — where Walde's generation identified it, Jankovics & Monok catalogued it as D-61, ‘copiously annotated, except for the last 200 pages’, and the house read it on the scan on 19 October 2026. Eleven years after the gift Tycho, at Benátky, was handed the catalogue of Dudith's whole library by Karl von Wartenberg and asked Monau whether it could be bought; Monau answered that it hung ‘between heaven and earth’, pawned and not the heirs' to sell. The dedication leaf was cut out of the volume at an unknown date (Haglund's note of 2 December 1963 records the loss), passed through Owen Gingerich's collection and was sold at Swann on 22 April 2025 — the earliest dated presentation of the book now in private hands, its host identified. Grade B: the attribution of the hand to Dudith is the Royal Library's and the reconstruction's, matched on the object by the number, the ink and the distribution of the notes, not yet by a comparison with a Dudith autograph (Jankovics & Monok 1993, no. 44 and pp. 9–15; the letters TBOO VII, 17 August 1588, 10 May and 1 November 1589, 16 May and 14 June 1600; census records stockholm-kb and private-dudith-fragment-leaf).

Bruno reads Tycho: the Acrotismus at Uraniborg and the De immenso (1588–1591)

1588–1591ScienceBrecep-bruno-acrotismus-1588

Giordano Bruno had no place in Tycho's correspondence and never came to Hven, yet a copy of his Camoeracensis acrotismus (Wittenberg 1588) reached Uraniborg with a dedication to Tycho, and the copy survives in the Clementinum (see the Bruno volume in the Bibliotheca). How the book travelled is not settled: Kořán (1969) and Horský (1975) proposed Hagecius at Prague as the intermediary; Sturlese (1985, pp. 324–325) objected that only one letter passed between Hagecius and Tycho in 1588 and suggested the same person who had given Bruno the first Uraniborg print, Morsing's Diarium of the comet of 1585 (Westman 2011, pp. 304–305 and p. 568 n. 127). In the De immenso et innumerabilibus (Frankfurt 1591) Bruno names Tycho twice: on the comets, where he writes that he had reached his conclusions by physical reasoning years before and now found them confirmed by the learned Tycho the Dane, whose sagacious genius had found and laid open many things agreeing with these senses — ‘docti accipio firmata Tichonis / Servatis Dani, ingenio qui multa sagaci / Invenit, atque aperit conformia sensibus hisce’ (book I, chapter 5) — and again on the proper motion of the stars (book IV, chapter 9) (Westman, p. 305 and p. 569 nn. 132–134, after Tessicini 2007). Bruno also knew of ‘a round comet’ of 1585 and of ‘a new star in the months of October and November’ that ‘Uraniborg astronomers’ had placed above Saturn, a report Westman leaves undecided between Kassel and Uraniborg as its source (p. 305 n. 134); Morsing's astrological section on the comet Bruno ignored (Spruit 2002, cited p. 569 n. 137). The Latin is quoted from Westman, who prints it from the Opera latine; the edition itself has not been consulted.

The world systems compared in print: Ursus 1588 and Roeslin 1597

1588–1597ScienceBrecep-world-systems-in-print-1597

Ursus's Fundamentum astronomicum (Strasbourg, end of July 1588) carried a planetary diagram equivalent in its geometry to Wittich's ‘sphere of revolutions’ of 1578, with the Earth turning daily, and appeared within months of Tycho's De mundi (Westman 2011, p. 293). Helisaeus Roeslin's De opere Dei creationis (Frankfurt 1597) was the first book to set the competing arrangements side by side, each as numbered propositions with a woodcut plate — Ptolemy, Copernicus, Ursus, Tycho and Roeslin's own — and the plates were mislabelled by the Frankfurt publisher: the Copernican system stands under Tycho's name with a printed apology beneath, Ursus's diagram under Copernicus's, Tycho's under Ursus's (pp. 342–347, figs 70–72; p. 574 n. 40, which also notes that Ursus in 1597 ‘shifted his position toward that of Tycho and accepted the intersection of the paths of Mars and the Sun’). Roeslin had borrowed Maestlin's copy of De mundi for two weeks at the end of 1588 and concluded that Ursus, Tycho's pupil, had taken the arrangement and added the Earth's daily motion so as not to seem to agree with him: ‘iudico Raymarum sua habere a Tychone (cuius discipulus fuit) et terram ille mobilem statuit, ne videatur cum Tychone consentire’ (Württembergische Landesbibliothek, Cod. Math. 4° 14b, fol. 19r; Granada 1996, pp. 119–120, as quoted by Westman, p. 566 n. 58); he also doubted that any astronomer had seen Mars's parallax exceed the Sun's (p. 342). The Frankfurt spring catalogue of 1597 announced Kepler's Mysterium, Tycho's Epistolae astronomicae and Roeslin's De opere Dei together (Fabian, Die Messkataloge des sechzehnten Jahrhunderts V, pp. 370, 372, 373; p. 573 n. 25); Ursus's reply, De astronomicis hypothesibus (Prague 1597), built on passages of the Epistolae, called Tycho ‘a mere mechanic’ and Rothmann ‘Rotzmann’ (p. 348). For the suit that followed, see the quarrel with Ursus.

Willem Blaeu, Tycho’s pupil, maps Hven’s stars onto the celestial globe (1596–1600)

1596–1600SkyArecep-blaeu-globes

Willem Janszoon Blaeu — about twenty-five — wintered on Hven in 1595–96, learning Tycho’s instruments and observing methods at first hand. He carried that apprenticeship back to Amsterdam: the celestial globes issued from his shop from about 1600 were mapped on Tycho’s star positions, and Christianson notes that such globes ‘using Tycho’s stellar positions,’ from Blaeu, the Langrens and Jodocus Hondius, ‘set a new standard for celestial cartography.’ Blaeu had observed enough on the island to discover a new star in Cygnus — P Cygni, ‘Blaeu’s Star,’ of 1600 — which he marked on his globes. The pupil became the founder of the Blaeu cartographic house; his son Joan would later crown the line with the coloured Tycho instrument plates of the 1664 Grooten Atlas (read elsewhere in this collection). It is the most direct carrying of Hven’s sky into the European map of the heavens.

The ruin and the quarry (1597–1652)

1597–1652SiteBrecep-hven-ruin-quarried

Tycho took his instruments off Hven in 1597 and the island reverted to the Crown's disposal. Faxe's reading of what followed is that neither building was merely left uncared for but that both were destroyed by design — the evidence being that Huet found no trace of Uraniborg in 1652, and that the walls of the Kungsgård raised a little distance away under Christian IV are built of the stone of Tycho's castle. By 1823 the site of Uraniborg was an open square of some 160 alnar to a side, ringed by rampart-like rises with four half-circles running out of them, and Stjerneborg betrayed itself only as a patch of ground lying slightly lower than the land around.

A contemporary Danish verdict — Tycho's pomp at Wandsbek (1598)

1598BiographyBrecep-stygge-1598

The earliest sustained contemporary character-portrait of Tycho is a hostile one. In a Latin letter of 1598 — written just after Tycho left Denmark, by the Danish nobleman Johan Stygge (so identified in the modern edition; the editor C. F. Bricka had cautiously left it as 'Hans or Johan Stygge') and addressed to the Copenhagen bookseller Hans Aalborg, 'the most erudite Master Johannes Alburgiacus' — the writer reports the talk circulating abroad about the astronomer's departure and rebuts it from the Danish-establishment side. He records the partisans' lament that 'Denmark, deprived of both eyes, is now made blind' (Daniam utroqve orbatam oculo … coecam nunc redditam), that the loss was a relapse into 'a new barbarism from which the Muses are exiled' (novam barbariem, ex qva exulavére Musæ), and that others blamed Machiavellism, envy or avarice at court — only to insist how slight a loss it truly was 'that our country lost this Tycho and was unwilling to keep him.' The portrait that follows is the document's real value: Tycho, he writes, 'is still in a certain Rantzau castle not far off' (in arce Ranzoviana qvadam non longe hinc remota — i.e. Wandsbek), but 'very rarely enters the town; and when he comes, he arrives with so many carriages and horses, and so pompously, as if he were more than noble.' He 'no longer acknowledges his own countrymen and scarcely wishes to be held a Dane' (suos conterraneos non amplius agnoscit vixqve Danus haberi vult) — refusing even to speak once with his own cousin Jost Høg (consobrino Jodoco Hogio) — and rides through the streets behind six horses with a great crowd running to see him 'as if he were a prince' (ac si princeps esset), with silk cloth and cushions laid on his reserved seat when, rarely, he enters church. Whatever its malice, the letter is the contemporary documentary ancestor of the question J. A. Fridericia would argue at the 1901 tercentenary — that Tycho's own conduct, not merely a hostile court, drove his fall from royal favour. The same document survives in the Epistolarium as Johan Stygge's letter to Hans Aalborg of 10 July 1598 (brahe-t_15980710001; Dreyer, Opera Omnia VIII, from the Rigsarkivet copy) — the character-portrait and its critical text one cross-reference apart. A single partisan voice, to be read against the friendlier evidence; not a balanced verdict.

Gilbert answers Tycho's cannon: De magnete (1600) and De mundo (1651)

1600–1651ScienceBrecep-gilbert-cannon-1600

In De magnete (London 1600), book VI, William Gilbert rebutted the thought experiment Tycho had set against the Earth's daily rotation in De mundi — the cannon fired east and west — and concluded that the diurnal motion ‘is not at all refuted by the illustrious Tycho Brahe through such arguments as these’ (De magnete, p. 341; Westman 2011, pp. 370–372, fig. 73 and p. 580 nn. 145–146). Gilbert knew Ursus's Fundamentum and Tycho's De mundi but shows no acquaintance with the Epistolae astronomicae or with the quarrel of 1597, and he did not, it seems, take Mars's parallax on Tycho's authority (p. 372 and n. 154). The posthumous De mundo nostro sublunari (Amsterdam 1651) describes the Tychonic ordering: ‘Illustrissimus Tycho Brahe Solem vult centrum esse secundorum mobilium, sive planetarum, terram vero constituit centrum universi; Mercurium & Venerem Copernici ratione circa Solem cieri’ (ed. 1965, p. 192; p. 580 n. 147). There is no evidence that Tycho saw De magnete before his death in 1601; Kepler had his copy by November 1602 and read Gilbert as the answer to the cannon (p. 372 and n. 159). Galileo's copy, with his provenance, is Biblioteca Nazionale Centrale di Firenze B.R.121 (fig. 74, p. 373).

The afterlife of the Tychonic world-system

17th c.ScienceBrecep-tychonic-system

The Tychonic world-system's chief textbook vehicle was Longomontanus's Astronomia Danica (1622; see the Astronomia Danica episode); among the Jesuits it found its great champion in G. B. Riccioli, whose Almagestum Novum (1651) — the same work that fixed the name of the lunar crater Tycho — argued at length for a semi-Tychonic variant. The system's eighteenth-century diffusion can be seen in Danish museum impressions: Homann's 'Systema Mundi Tychonicum' after Doppelmayr (Nuremberg, first 1712, printed 1742; Maritime Museum 1996:0561) and the 1742 Atlas coelestis plate with the three systems in a corner (1996:0564), a French 'Sisteme de Ticho Brahé' among the systems of the world on a Dutch-privileged sheet (1958:0143), and, earlier, the woodcut 'Sphera Tychonis Brahæ' in Frederik Bolling's Oost-Indiske Reise-bog of 1678 (1950:0311). At Prague the system was a Jesuit showpiece: Keyssler saw in the Clementinum's museum in 1730 ‘a great armillary sphere which moves according to the system of Tyge Brahe’, Klein's clock of 1751 carried a model of it on its back, and Strnad's inventory of 1781 lists a ‘Systema Jovis ad mentem Tychonis’ (Seydl 1951, pp. 7, 15, 48; see the Clementinum's ‘Tychonic’ pieces). The earliest reader's response in a reader's own hand is Andreas Dudith's, in red ink in the margins of the copy Tycho sent him in May 1588 — ‘terra immota; reliq. 5 pl[ane]tae circa ☉ gyrantur’ under the diagram of p. 189 (Stockholm, RAR 106 A; see Dudith reads De mundi).

The naked-eye precision tradition — Hevelius and Verbiest

17th c.ScienceBrecep-naked-eye-tradition

The last practitioner of Tychonic naked-eye precision was Johannes Hevelius (1611–1687) of Danzig, who as late as the 1660s compiled a star catalogue of high accuracy using 'Tychonic' instruments without telescopic sights (Wolfschmidt, Crossroads, p. 211). When Robert Hooke insisted that only telescopic sights and micrometer eyepieces could now give adequate accuracy, Hevelius defended the naked-eye method — a defence descending directly from Tycho's rimula-and-transversal protocol — and to adjudicate the dispute the Royal Society sent the young Edmond Halley to Danzig in 1679; Halley was impressed by Hevelius's accuracy, though later retrospective comparison favoured the telescopic instruments (Biographical Encyclopedia, p. 502). The tradition reached China, where the Jesuit Ferdinand Verbiest built six bronze instruments for the Peking Imperial Observatory in 1675 after the plates of the Mechanica — direct descendants of Tycho's designs (du Halde 1736); four of them were carried off to Potsdam by German troops in 1901 and returned after the First World War (Mortensen 1942, p. 21). Naked-eye sights gave way to telescopic sights with Flamsteed at Greenwich.

The grave in the Týn Church, Prague

1601 – presentLegacyBextantrecep-grave-prague

Tycho was buried in 1601 in the Church of Our Lady before Týn on Prague's Old Town Square, his tomb marked by a relief effigy in armour. The grave itself, as the opening of 1901 found it: under the stone paving of the church, beneath the marble gravestone set in the floor with the epitaph above it, a brick vault over an oblong rectangular chamber, broken in on its west side and filled with gravel; the man's burial on the right, the southern side, directly at the foot of the gravestone, the woman's on the left (Matiegka 1901, printed p. 4, scan 4). Gravestone and epitaph were restored in 1901 at the city council's expense, which was the occasion of the opening (unnumbered opening leaf, scan 3); the floor had been relaid in 1721 at the cost of many gravestones (scan 3). The regulations of 1901 allowed neither the removal of remains from the church nor a reburial after removal (footnote, printed p. 13, scan 13); on 29 July 1901 the tin coffin was laid at the same place of honour in the refurbished vault (printed p. 14, scan 14), the woman's bones having been left in place (printed p. 13).

The unpaid 20,000 daler — the heirs' thirty-year claim on the imperial treasury (1601–1630)

1601–1630LegacyBrecep-heirs-unpaid-claim

Rudolf II's promises to provide for Tycho and his family 'seem to have gone to the grave with him' (Friis). The heirs recovered his salary arrears only with great difficulty — and, on the Emperor's order, computed only to the day of death, 24 October 1601. For the instruments the purchase price was fixed by agreement at 20,000 daler, but payment barely came: about 5,000 daler at once, then instalments with interest totalling some 2,300 more up to 1613 — and after that, apparently, nothing. A dispute between the Emperor and the Bohemian estates over who should pay (the estates arguing the Emperor had bought the instruments 'for his own pleasure') left the balance hanging. After the Battle of the White Mountain (November 1620) the Protestant family had to leave Bohemia — except the son-in-law Tengnagel, by then an imperial councillor, and Jørgen Brahe, who was permitted to remain expressly to press the family's money claim against Ferdinand II. On 18 December 1628 Ferdinand rescribed from Vienna to the royal Rentekammer in Prague that the matter be settled with all speed, and the file went to the Reformation Commissioners; the outcome is unknown — no receipt from the heirs has ever been found. That the balance was still unpaid on 3 May 1630 is shown by a document of that date, drawn up in German by Sophie Brahe and the Danish kin, formally recognising Tycho's children as legitimate and their mother Christine as his lawful wife, for use in their inheritance suits — the same family arvesag to which the surviving children's letter printed by Friis in Danske Magazin (5. R. II, pp. 264–266) belongs. Figures partly 'according to other statements' (Friis); graded B accordingly.

The fluid heaven in English: Heydon's Defence of Judiciall Astrologie (1603)

Sir Christopher Heydon's Defence of Judiciall Astrologie (Cambridge 1603), written against John Chamber, takes its astronomy from the Epistolae astronomicae and the Progymnasmata: ‘our modern Mathematickes’ had proved ‘but one onely continued substance from the concaue superficies of the Moone, to the 8. It is the earliest English printing of Tycho's fluid heaven and of the Progymnasmata's ‘newe Hypotheses’, two years after his death.

The Tychonic hypothesis among the moderns, 1644–1674: Descartes, Wilkins, Wing, Hooke

1644–1674ScienceBrecep-moderns-1644-1674

Descartes's Principia philosophiae (Amsterdam 1644) sets the Tychonic hypothesis beside the Copernican and claims to ‘deny the motion of the Earth more carefully than Copernicus and more truthfully than Tycho’; from the vortices he deduces ‘the physical impossibility of the Sun's carrying with it all the planets except the Earth’, and of the comet observers, Tycho among them, he writes that ‘they did not dare to attribute to them the altitude which their calculations were revealing’ (Westman 2011, pp. 497–498 and p. 600 nn. 75–78). Wilkins, in the Discourse concerning a New Planet (1640), explained Tycho's resistance to Copernicus by the rule that ‘Every Man is naturally more affected to his own Brood, than to that of which another is the Author’ (p. 498 n. 84). Vincent Wing built his first compendium on ‘the tables of Tycho, Argoli, and Lansbergen’ and by 1669 dismissed Tycho's hypotyposis as ‘more ingenuous than true’ (p. 503 nn. 112, 116); Gassendi adhered, ‘at least outwardly’, to the Tychonic arrangement (p. 500 n. 91); Riccioli taught the semi-Tychonic at Parma (p. 499 n. 89; see the afterlife of the world-system). Hooke's Attempt to Prove the Motion of the Earth (London 1674) framed the stellar parallax as ‘an experimentum crucis to determine between the Tychonick and Copernican Hypotheses’, asked whether there was not ‘much reason for the Hypothesis of Ticho at least, when he with all the accurateness that he arrived to with his vast Instruments, or Riccioli, who pretends much to out-strip him, were not able to find any sensible Parallax of the Earths Orb among the fixt Stars’, and proposed a telescope better than their instruments (pp. 508–509 and nn. 140–143). Westman's gloss: the naked eye cannot distinguish an angle of less than a minute, so ‘Tycho and Riccioli thus claimed more than they could deliver’ (p. 509).

The observation archive → Copenhagen → the Opera Omnia

1655 / 1913–29DataAextantrecep-observations-opera-omnia

The fate of the observation logs. Bought by the Emperor with the instruments, they were lent to Kepler and kept after his death by his son Ludwig Kepler as a pawn against the unpaid imperial debt (a 1633 bond of 12,694 gulden). With no prospect of payment, Ludwig sold them in 1655 to King Frederik III of Denmark for 600 rixdollars; they have been in Det Kongelige Bibliotek, Copenhagen, ever since, and were published by J. L. E. Dreyer as Tomes X–XIII of the Tychonis Brahe Opera Omnia (1913–1929). Had Kepler's pledge to return them to the imperial court been kept, Werlauff observed, they would have ended in Vienna, not Copenhagen. What Kepler's slip pasted on the 1596–97 volume actually undertakes is told three ways: Werlauff read it as a promise to return the books to the imperial court; the 1612 declaration, as Dreyer prints it, has each volume marked as Kepler's treasure and locked away should he die suddenly; Mortensen paraphrases it as an undertaking to keep the heirs informed of deaths in the Kepler family (Mortensen 1942, pp. 18–19) — three paraphrases of one slip, recorded until the slip is read. Mortensen counts the Royal Library's set as '10 Bind i Folio og 8 i Kvart'.

Joan Blaeu’s Grooten Atlas — Tycho’s instruments in colour for a vernacular public (1664)

1664LegacyArecep-blaeu-atlas-1664

Joan Blaeu reproduced all twenty-two of Tycho Brahe’s principal instrument illustrations from the Astronomiae Instauratae Mechanica (Wandsbek, 1598) as richly coloured engravings in his Grooten Atlas oft Werelt-beschryving (Amsterdam, 1664) — nine volumes in Dutch, the largest and most expensive atlas ever produced at that date. The atlas’s Denmark section spans 91 pages (pp. 136–226); its core is the 53-page treatment of Hven, which includes the Uraniborg architectural plans (garden, elevation, floor plan), the Stjerneborg bird’s-eye and floor plan, and Dutch vernacular descriptions of every major instrument — the first presentation of Tycho’s observational apparatus to a broad non-Latin reading public; the preceding pages on Denmark, Jutland, Funen, the islands and Zealand set the geographical context. The series includes the coloured interior view of Uraniborg (p. 201), showing Tycho himself with three assistants at the mural quadrant. Blaeu also reproduces the Stjerneborg inscriptions, including the salutation that closes the crypt-entrance text: ‘Have qui haec legis facisque, et vale’ (Hail, you who read and do these things — and farewell). The surviving Utrecht copy (Universiteitsbibliotheek Utrecht, AC 62 Rariora) is one of the principal sources for the coloured instrument tradition.

Historia Coelestis (Augsburg 1666) — the observations first in print

1666DataArecep-historia-coelestis-1666

The editio princeps of the bulk of Tycho's reduced annual observation logs (1582–1601). It was edited from manuscript transcripts in the Vienna Hofbibliothek by the Jesuit Albert Curtz (Curtius) under the anagrammatic pseudonym 'Lucius Barrettus', printed at Augsburg by Simon Utzschneider on the imperial commission of Ferdinand III. The text is unreliable — its omissions and printer's errors were catalogued at length by Erasmus Bartholin (1668) and again by Dreyer (1890); Bartholin's own corrected exemplars survive as the KB volumes GKS 308–309. Some copies carry a cancel title 'Ratisbonae 1672' (1666 sheets reissued at Regensburg). Superseded only by Dreyer's Opera Omnia X–XIII (1913–29), which printed the observations from the Copenhagen autographs (see the Opera Omnia edition of the observations, below). The edition's apparatus includes an engraved plate of the ‘seats of Brahean astronomy’, facing a lettered key: in the centre Uraniborg within its walled garden, ‘the first and principal seat’, and in four roundels Wandsbek castle, ‘arx Ranzoviana’, dated 1597; the castle of Benátky in Bohemia, 1598; the Emperor's Prague gardens (the Belvedere), 1600; and the house of Jacob Curtius of Senftenau, vice-chancellor of the Empire, ‘the last seat’, 1601 — the itinerary of the exile as the editors of 1666 fixed it, sixty-five years on, with the dates the Loca pillar also carries. The Benátky roundel is unrealistic (so already the National Library's caption: ‘nerealistický’). Reproduced from the National Library of the Czech Republic's copy, 14 B 47/P.1, by Vackářová 2026 (printed p. 30 (scan 30), Obr. 39).

For two centuries Danish almanacs and chapbooks listed the year's unlucky days as 'Tycho Brahes Dage' — thirty-two days on which nothing of weight should be begun — and hung on them a legend that the list had been found in a wall of Esrum Abbey in 1616 'efter velbyrdige Tychu Brahe'. The Clementinum's ‘Tychonic’ pieces — Klein's Tychonic clock, the planetaria ‘probabiliter a Tychone’, the sextants shown as Tycho's, and the sale that scattered them (1730–1909)

1730–1909LegacyBextantrecep-clementinum-tychonica

At Prague the Tychonic system outlived its science as a showpiece of the Jesuit college of St Clement, and the college's Mathematical Museum (in being in some form by 1638, when the Ryff Sammelband NK 14 E 3 was inscribed ‘Collegii Caesarei Soctis JESU Pragae Musaei Mathem: A.1638’ — Mačák 1995/96, printed p. 74 (scan 2), who reads the inscription, printed as his type, as showing a museum ‘in some form’ in the first half of the seventeenth century; formally founded 1722, dissolved 1785) hung Tycho's name on a set of objects the eighteenth century took for his. Keyssler saw there on 11 October 1730 ‘a great armillary sphere which moves according to the system of Tyge Brahe’; about 1751 Johann Klein built the clock called ‘Tychonic’, its back a model of the planetary system ‘according to Tyge Brahe's conception’, shown to the academic senate at the Carolinum and described with plates by Stepling the same year, with a ‘Copernican’ pendant in 1752 — the clock Strnad's inventory of 1781 lists as ‘Horologium Tychonis Brahe’ (D 3), exhibited today in the Clementinum's Baroque Hall. The same inventory holds a ‘Systema Jovis ad mentem Tychonis’ among three armillary models ‘adhuc probabiliter a Tychone’ (C 17–C 18 bis), a ‘Sextans Tychonis’ (D 8) and a Habermel instrument ‘pro Tychone’ (C 51), and visitors had been shown ‘a small iron octant said to have belonged to Tycho’ since at least 1769. After the dissolution the pieces passed to the observatory, where director Böhm sold the ‘unusable’ old instruments in 1852–56 — 45 sundials, three of them Habermel's gilt pieces of 1598 and 1600, 18 telescopes, quadrants and sextants, six planetaria, nine globes — for a few florins; the two more complex of the three ‘Tychonic’ models resurfaced abroad as ‘new finds’ (Das Weltall, 1904), were bought by the painter Gabriel Max and given in 1909 to the Deutsches Museum in Munich, where Mortensen found a planetarium engraved ‘Ty. de Brahe’ and a sundial ‘Insula Heveen A. D. 1578’ in a case with Tycho's name and arms in glass mosaic — the engravings forgeries made, he supposed, to raise the price after the sale, the attribution beneath them Strnad's ‘probabiliter’ of 1781 and nothing older. Böhm rebutted the attributions in 1857; in 1868 the Vienna government asked after a sold ‘Tychonic astrolabe’ and was told the pieces had gone ‘without a prior inspection’. The two iron sextants stayed in Prague and are the Národní technické muzeum's today; how they entered the Jesuit museum Seydl, the observatory's own historian, declares unknown (Seydl 1951, pp. 6–7, 14–18, 22–26, 30–36, 48–49, figs 7–8, 15–16, 19; Mortensen 1942, pp. 21–22; Hyklová 2017, p. 2).

The cellar under the plough, and the cellar that was not beneath it (1747–1824)

1747–1824SiteBrecep-hven-cellar-1747

A cellar at Uraniborg came to light in 1747 when a horse in a plough-team fell through its failing vault and it was found that the field had been ploughed over a cellar; it had lain under the rooms Tycho's servants occupied. From the hollow sound the floor returned when struck — the same sound heard against its north wall — Sjöborg conjectured in 1815 that a further cellar lay beneath, and the belief became general among visitors to the site. Ekdahl dug under it and beside it in 1824 and found neither cellar nor room: islanders had been digging there in secret for money, and it is the loosened earth, not a void, that answers differently to the blow.

The first Danish memorial to Tycho is a design by Johannes Wiedewelt engraved by J. F. The ruins made an undisturbed common (1805–1824)

1805–1824SiteArecep-hven-protection-1805

At the enskifte conference on Hven in 1805 it was resolved that the remains of Tycho Brahe's castle 'skola, såsom en åminnelse, blifva en orubbad allmänning' — should remain an undisturbed common, as a memorial (§8) — and that no plot be taken there for a school or poorhouse, the castle itself being thought to serve (§13). Nothing was ever built on it. The leaseholders Arvid Schau and, until his death in 1820, Pehr Otto Tausson kept the ground from damage; Tausson planted trees where the King and Queen had taken a meal during their visit in the summer of 1804, of which little remained twenty years later. Once the digging began the county governor placed the site under formal protection.

The first excavation of Stjerneborg and Uraniborg (1823–24)

1823–1824SiteArecep-ekdahl-excavation-1823

Ditching to mend the road that runs between the two sites exposed a granite column at midsummer 1823. Nils Johan Ekdahl, then pastor's adjunct at St Ibb, dug where Stjerneborg had stood — almost alone, Faxe records, and seldom helped by more than one workman — following Tycho's own Ichnographia Stelleburgi. Winter stopped the work; it resumed through the summer of 1824 and was still running that October. The crypts, the connecting passages and the square hypocaustum came to light, each answering to its letter on Tycho's plan, together with the places of his own bed and of the pupils'. At Uraniborg the well, whose position was not known, was found after some days' digging, cleaned out and returned to use; the chemical laboratory, a deep cellar of retorts and sublimated sulphur, and the floor of the printing house were located. It is the first archaeological excavation of either building.

Tycho's comets recomputed — the 19th-century orbit reductions (1854)

1844–1854ScienceArecep-comet-recomputation-19c

Two and a half centuries after Tycho recorded them, his cometary observations were still good enough to yield modern orbits. The seven comets entered in his working brouillon (GKS 1826, 1577–1596) were re-reduced one by one by the astronomers of the mid-nineteenth century — F. W. Woldstedt took the comet of 1577 (Helsingfors, 1844; Dreyer 1890, p. 172 n. 3), J. R. Hind those of 1582 and 1590 (the latter in the Monthly Notices of the Royal Astronomical Society 7, 1846), C. A. F. Peters that of 1585 (Astronomische Nachrichten 39, 1849; Thoren 1990, p. 203 n.), and H. C. F. C. Schjellerup, observer at the Copenhagen University observatory, that of 1580 (1854) — Schjellerup's own preface (pp. 3–4) records the division of labour. Schjellerup's memoir 'Tycho Brahes Original-Observationer, benyttede til Banebestemmelse af Cometen 1580' (Videnskabernes Selskabs Skrifter, 1854) worked straight from Tycho's autograph sextant observations at the Royal Library — the quarto brouillon, with the 1670 Bartholin–Rømer fair copies beside it — learning to read Tycho's notoriously disorderly hand (the numerals always clean) by collating it against Schumacher's edition of the comet of 1585. It is the nineteenth-century forerunner of the way Tycho's data are still used as working science today (see 'The data still in use', below): the same archive, reduced by each age to the precision of its own methods. This whole recomputation campaign preceded — and partly motivated — Dreyer's printing of the observation logs in the Opera Omnia (1913–29).

Dreyer, Tycho Brahe (Edinburgh 1890) — the foundational biography

1890BiographyArecep-dreyer-biography-1890

J. L. E. Dreyer's Tycho Brahe: A Picture of Scientific Life and Work in the Sixteenth Century (Edinburgh: Adam & Charles Black, 1890) is the first biography to rest on systematic archival research rather than on the traditions flowing from Gassendi. Dreyer had access to the Copenhagen manuscripts (the observation logs, the correspondence), Tycho's printed works, and the Hven records; his book covers the life, the instruments (with the GKS observation-volume catalogue that remains the backbone of the KB–Vienna collation), the star catalogue, the planetary theories, and the world-system. It superseded Gassendi's Vita (1654) as the authoritative life and remains the essential primary reference for Tycho's chronology, instruments, and scientific context. Its limitations: it antedates the prosopographical deepening of Christianson (2000) and the technical reassessment of Thoren (1990); the Tychonic system and its rivals are treated from a late-Victorian scientific vantage.

'Species Tychonis Brahe' — his name in the pharmacy

20th century (the objects)LegacyCextantrecep-pharmacopoeia

Three objects carry his name into the chemist's shop. Among forty-two green-painted tin canisters from a Danish pharmacy, each labelled 'Ikke til medicinsk brug' and numbered under the pharmacy's control, Den Gamle By holds one lettered 'SPECIES TYCHO BRAHE' (01834:74); Østfyns Museer another, 'Species Tychonis Brahe K.A. 22' (06091X0159); and Museum Lolland-Falster a prescription for 'Tycho Brahes dråber' (LFS18070). A species is a dried herbal mixture; that one was sold under Tycho's name reflects the fame of his medicines — the elixir against plague he gave away on Hven and the pharmaceutical recipes that circulated after him — but which preparation these tins held, when it entered the Danish pharmacopoeia and what it claimed to owe him have not been examined. Grade C: entered on the museums' records alone, as a thread to be read.

The 1901 exhumation (the tercentenary)

1901LegacyArecep-exhumation-1901

The opening of 1901 began as a restoration. Early in 1901 the committee preparing the tercentenary of Tycho's death, falling on 24 October 1901, asked the city council of Prague to restore the marble gravestone and the epitaph above it in the Týn church; the architect and k. k. Konservator J. Herain, who supervised that work, proposed opening the supposed vault to settle whether the body was still there at all. The doubt had two grounds: after the Battle of the White Mountain in 1620 the bodies of non-Catholics were removed from the church — Tycho's name is not among those recorded — and the relaying of the stone floor in 1721, which cost many gravestones and probably graves, could have displaced any remains (Matiegka 1901, unnumbered opening leaf, scan 3). The council consented and paid; digging began on the morning of 24 June under Herain, with the parish priest P. Knobloch, the k. k. Konservator B. Jelínek (not further identified, and not to be merged on the surname with the observatory's Jelinek of the inquiries of 1857 and 1868) and the councillor W. Brož present. When the clothing and beard remains of the southern body suggested the astronomer, the search was halted until 26 June, when the two experts, Professor A. Schrutz and the Universitätsdocent H. Matiegka, were called in; it continued on 27 June (printed p. 4, scan 4). Matiegka reports the bodily remains only; the archaeological findings he refers to a report Herain was preparing for the k. k. Central-Commission in Vienna (printed pp. 4–5). He does not date the breach in the vault: the broken vault and its fill are, in his words, what later confirmed the doubts (scan 3). Two burials lay in the vault. The man, on the right-hand, southern side, directly at the foot of the gravestone, rested in a double coffin lined with a light silk shroud, by then much decayed; his head, on a cushion, wore a velvet beret with a much-damaged bronze-wire clasp, a dark-red silk coat covered the body, and the feet were in knee-length thread stockings and shoes. The woman, on the left, lay in a single wooden coffin with no trace of clothing and about two hundred white pierced bone beads on her crossed hands; her sex was recognised only afterwards. The vault's gravel fill, mixed with fragments of the coffin lids, thinned towards the east end but covered everything (printed p. 4, scan 4). Before the skeleton was touched its length was taken in situ: from the heel to the presumed crown, judged from the lie of the cap since the vault of the skull was missing, 168 to 170 cm; from the heel to the upper edge of the pubic symphysis, which had kept its place, 92·5 cm (printed p. 5, scan 5). The skeleton lay extended, the right elbow bent acutely with the hand on the upper chest, the left at a right angle with the hand somewhat lower; loose hand bones lay over the upper abdomen; feet and lower legs were still in the stockings. Of the right arm the humerus had decayed to a remnant of its head and the ulna in part; the rest, above all the spine and pelvis, was well preserved, every bone strong and massive with heavy muscle markings, the breastbone strikingly broad in its lower part. The experts did the cleaning and lifting with their own hands (printed p. 5). The long bones, measured after Manouvrier: left humerus 339 mm, left radius 251, left ulna 271; left femur 485 (greatest length) and 484 (in position), right femur 485 and 483; left and right tibia 384 each — measures that, Matiegka says, agree on average with the stature taken in situ (printed p. 6, scan 6). The skull remains were lifted whole on a metal plate slid beneath them, together with the cap, the clothing round them, the cushion and the earth, and photographed from the front and the side before the clothing was removed (printed p. 6, scan 6). Only part of the face had survived: part of the frontal bone to a little above the glabella, the nasal bones, the left cheekbone, most of the left and a smaller part of the right upper jaw with their teeth — the four incisors, both canines, both premolars on each side and the three left molars. All were heavily worn except the two left premolars, which Matiegka explains by a defective lower dentition; of the lower jaw nothing remained. Of the rest of the skull there was nothing but a grey, ash-like detritus and a few tufts of hair, mostly in the cap, with some firmer lumps resembling what he had seen in ossuary skulls and taken for saponified brain (printed pp. 6–7). Herain established that the breach in the vault lay directly over this head: the falling debris had crushed the skull, and the face survived only because the head had been strongly inclined forward; the damp of the heaped gravel completed the destruction (footnote, printed p. 7, scan 7). Soft tissue survived as thin crumbly brown layers on the long bones and as a coating on the face, in which remains of dense light-brown eyebrows sat on the orbital rims and, above the teeth, the remains of a mighty moustache. The detached half of it is 10·5 cm long as the page is best read — it prints 10, a gap and 5 cm with the middle dot dropped, and the text layer gives 105 — about 2 cm thick at its broadest, bluntly pointed and slightly S-curved; its hairs are up to 7–8·5 cm long, strongly twisted, brown with a violet tinge in places. The tufts from the back of the head, found mostly in the cap, are brown, somewhat reddish, lighter in places; hair lying in the earth changes colour, Matiegka notes, which he offers as the reason for the several shades (printed p. 7, scan 7). The facial fragment is of decidedly male type — strong brow ridges, massive bone, strong teeth, prominent cheekbones. The orbits are moderately oblique, 38 mm across by the French method, 41 by the German, 31 mm high; the upper face measures 81 mm from nasion to the incisal edge and 77 mm to the alveolar point (printed p. 7, scan 7). The nasal aperture is slightly asymmetric from a septum deviated moderately to the left; the anterior nasal spine is strongly developed. The nasal bones are 21·5 mm long and 13 mm wide at the root, 9 at the narrowest and 23 at the lower end; the aperture is 33 mm high and 27 wide; the nose 51 mm from nasion to spine — most of the nasal measures, Matiegka says, agree with the general means, the length of the nasal bones standing decidedly below the mean. The free lower edges of the nasal bones are thin and bent somewhat inward; in profile the middle of that edge tilts forward and down, so that the concave bridge ends abruptly in a small, more steeply set surface; seen from the front this is a narrow sickle-shaped strip bounding the aperture above, which under the lens shows sure signs of bone scarring, with a small tubercle above it that is visible from the side as well (printed p. 10, scan 10). The identification was a joint opinion: on the strength of the examination, and above all of the surviving parts of the face, the two experts called in to identify the body — Schrutz and Matiegka — gave the Gutachten that the body buried on the right-hand side under Tycho's gravestone was the astronomer's (printed p. 11, scan 11). Beside the archaeological findings they numbered six grounds: (1) the whole habitus of the skeleton and every bone, especially the face, is male and answers to the known portraits and to the gravestone over the vault; (2) the wear of the surviving teeth fits the age given for him, 55 years; (3) the skeletal measures answer to the stature of the life-size figure on the gravestone (printed p. 11); (4) the mighty moustache recalls the portraits, above all the one of 1586 (printed pp. 11–12); (5) the sickle-shaped surface and the small tubercle at the upper rim of the aperture are sure signs of reaction to an earlier injury, evidently the duel wound his biographers report, and the green stain shows that a copper-bearing body lay long on the bone (printed pp. 12–13); (6) the second skeleton is an older woman, probably his wife, who by the epitaph followed him in death after three years and was buried at his side (printed p. 13, scan 13). Šimůnek's summary of 2002 counts five — it omits the third and fourth grounds and splits the fifth; the page prints six. Ground 4 names the portrait the experts set the moustache against: the one Tycho added to the Stammbuch he dedicated to his son in 1598, which Matiegka describes as cut in wood by Geyn at Amsterdam in 1586, citing Studnička's reproduction in Prager Tychoniana (Prague 1901) (printed pp. 11–12, scans 11–12). Whether the attribution is Studnička's or his own the page does not say, and it is not adopted here — the de Gheyn plates of 1586 are engravings and the woodcut of 1586 is anonymous; which print he meant, and what the son's album carries, are the Iconographia's matter. What 1901 established about the nose is a stain and an inference. The light-green colour along the rim of the aperture, traced 7–15 mm onto the nasal bones and upper jaw, is of the kind found on grave bones that have lain under copper-bearing metal (printed pp. 10–11); from it Matiegka argues that a copper-bearing object lay on the bone for a long time after death and impregnated it with copper salts — which, he adds, is part of why this small piece of skull outlasted the rest. The object was, ‘as may be assumed with great probability’, the much-discussed prosthesis; what he calls certain is that the prosthesis, whether it had been given out as silver or gold, was very rich in copper. Tycho, versed in alloying metals and seeking a mixture that matched the skin without much painting over and was durable, would naturally have chosen a copper alloy; a nose worn on the face had to be light and therefore of thin sheet, which explains why oxidation and the pressure of the gravel destroyed it so completely that the most zealous search found no trace — the near-collapse of the sturdier clasp on the beret, whose parts threatened to crumble at a touch, speaks for the same process (printed pp. 12–13, scans 12–13). The report records no chemical analysis; the alloy was determined only by the campaign of 2010. Between the examination and the reburial the remains were kept apart. The examination of 26 and 27 June 1901 was made on the spot with the public excluded. The woman's bones were left in the vault, which was provisionally covered. Tycho's bones were laid together in a box and given to the parish priest to keep in the sacristy until the metal coffin was ready — no other arrangement was possible, a footnote explains, since the regulations in force allowed neither the removal of the remains from the church nor a reburial afterwards; the council, which spared no expense, took a lively interest throughout. The skull fragment alone Matiegka took into his own keeping, to soften the adhering matter and clean the nasal region; only then were the detailed findings on the nose possible, and platinotypes were made anew of the cleaned fragment. On 6 July, in the parish priest's presence, he placed the fragment with all its teeth and the surviving remnant of the beard in a glass vessel with a ground-in lid and sealed it airtight, glued and cemented (printed p. 13 and its footnote, scan 13). On 29 July all the bones, the sealed glass with the skull fragment, a second glass with the supposed saponified remains of the brain and the clothing found in the grave — except the samples handed to the municipal museum for safe keeping — were closed in the tin coffin made in the meantime and lettered ‘1601 Tycho Brahe 1901’, and in the presence of several members of the commission and guests were laid again at the same place of honour, now more securely, in the refurbished vault (printed pp. 13–14, scans 13–14). This settles the divergence between Šimůnek and Kølln: Matiegka himself records both — the clothing went back into the coffin, samples of it to the city museum — so the two no longer contradict each other; whether the pieces Kølln lists at the City Museum are those samples, or more than samples, the museum's inventory must show. The certificate of authenticity that the National Museum's account places in the coffin is not mentioned in Matiegka's report; the museum's account remains its only witness here. The second skeleton, on the man's left, measured 155 cm and was a woman's of advanced age: the sagittal and lambdoid sutures were fully closed and the coronal suture closed in its middle part and at the temples; the teeth were heavily worn, though only the upper left second and third molars and the upper right third were missing, the lower dentition being complete with the last right molar carious. Skull: greatest length 182 mm, breadth 147, height 122; face height nasion–menton 115; bizygomatic breadth 136; upper face 75 mm to the incisal edge and 70 to the alveolar point; bimaxillary breadth 105; nose 49 high and 25 wide; orbits 35 high, 38 wide by the French method and 40 by the German. The pelvis is female (printed p. 11, scan 11). The report was laid before the Royal Bohemian Society of Sciences on 11 October 1901. Later witnesses add to the record. Mortensen measured the monument at 2.03 × 1.15 m and gives the wife's height as 155 cm — the primary's own figure; casts of the monument and of the skull fragment were made for the collection at Rundetaarn; the City Museum of Prague received garments from the grave in 1901 — a beret of gold velvet, a sleeve of dark-red brocade, a white wool stocking and a very small shoe of brown velvet (Kølln 1995, p. 69) — kept in a cardboard box until 1939, when at Mortensen's initiative, through Professor Arnošt Kraus, they were sealed in an airtight glass vessel; small textile fragments taken from the grave per nefas were later found in several hands, some at the Treptow observatory in Berlin (Mortensen 1942, pp. 26–27). A plaster cast of the facial fragment, made in 1901 and given to Mortensen, was by 1959 at Frederiksborg (Paulli & Dal 1959, p. 8) — an object distinct from that museum's cast of the effigy. The 1901 statements above are read at the page of Matiegka's report (the house copy, read October 2026); the one statement still resting on a secondary account is the certificate of authenticity, known only from the National Museum's web account — the grade A given here covers the 1901 examination and the later witnesses read at their pages, not that sentence.

Tycho in Prague's memory, 1901–1984 — Studnička's Prager Tychoniana, the Copernicus year 1973 and the Vajce group on Pohořelec

1901–1984LegacyBextantrecep-prague-commemoration-1901-1984

The tercentenary of his death produced František Josef Studnička's Prager Tychoniana (Prague 1901), issued — its own title page says — for the approaching three-hundredth anniversary of the death: the first report in print of the Clementinum copy of De revolutionibus (Vackářová 2026, printed p. 31 (scan 31), Obr. 41), and the first gathering of the Prague relics — the Clementinum books, the Strahov Mechanica, the manuscripts he took for autograph (so Studnička himself, in the house copy). The Copernicus year 1973 brought Zdeněk Horský's facsimile of the Clementinum copy of 1566, printed in 1971 under the title that still gave the notes to Tycho (Cimelia Bohemica 16; the National Library's copy BMMS Facs F 158), and the Prague quincentenary exhibition in which that copy was the central exhibit, prized as from Tycho's estate and for its notes (printed pp. 8–10 (scans 8–10); she names neither venue nor organiser). In 1984 the group of Tycho and Kepler by Josef Vajce was unveiled on Pohořelec, on the site of the Kurz house where Tycho died — the house demolished, in the National Library's telling, when the Baroque fortifications were built — in front of the school that now bears Kepler's name (printed p. 30 (scan 30), Obr. 40 and its caption). Kølln gives the statue's placing as 1983 after the competition of 1971 (1995, p. 67); the Czech monument databases hrady.cz and drobnepamatky.cz (entry of 25 May 2018) agree with Vackářová on 1984 as the year of the unveiling before the Gymnázium Jana Keplera, so the two dates read as placing and unveiling. Grade B: a curator's account and two hobbyist registers, none of them citing a document of 1983 or 1984.

C. V. L. Charlier's excavation of the Uraniborg foundations in 1901 gave Charles Christensen the empirical floor for the measured reconstruction of 1921, and established that the plans printed in Tycho's own lifetime do not answer to what is in the ground. Further digging followed in 1924, and the museum was raised on the site at the turn of the 1930s. The Mechanica reprinted and translated — Hasselberg 1901, Ræder 1946, the Prague set of 1996

1901–1996BiographyAextantrecep-mechanica-editions

The book that described the instruments outlived them by four centuries in three anniversary editions. For the tercentenary of Tycho's death Bernhard Hasselberg had the Wandsbek first edition reproduced in facsimile (Stockholm 1901) and in 1904 published the first census of its copies — thirty-three, most in public libraries. For the quatercentenary of his birth the Royal Danish Academy commissioned the first translation: Hans Ræder's unpublished Danish rendering turned into English with Elis and Bengt Strömgren (Copenhagen 1946) — the instrument chapters, the curriculum vitae and the appendices, without the preface, the poems or the letters. For the 450th anniversary the Prague classicists Alena Hadravová and Petr Hadrava, with Jole Shackelford, issued a three-part set (KLP, 1996): a colour facsimile of the Křivoklát presentation copy, the first complete Czech translation, and Ræder's English revised against the Latin with 301 notes and, for the first time in English, the preface to Rudolf II. Its Prologue is also the fullest printed account of the three Bohemian copies of 1598 and the only one of the Czech copies of 1602 and 1611. The poems and the Kurtz and Magini letters remain untranslated; the Latin with a Danish translation is now on tekstnet (2019).

The Danish memorials of the 1930s — Harald Mortensen's Tycho

1932–1957LegacyBextantrecep-danish-memorials-1930s

The Danish commemoration of Tycho between the wars was largely one man's work. Harald Mortensen (1878–1957), kustode at the Royal Library, founded and ran the astronomical-historical collection at Rundetaarn, and on his initiative Siegfried Wagner's bust of Tycho in grey syenite was raised there and unveiled on 8 August 1932, modelled on the watercolour portrait in the Strahov Mechanica (Mortensen 1942, p. 25). He had Sophie Brahe's grave at Torrlösa marked with a stone and set a memorial stone to Longomontanus in his birthplace, Lomborg; he arranged the local Tycho Brahe museum on Hven; and his obituarists credit him with rescuing the grave relics in Prague when they were perishing from neglect in the 1930s. His collection of Tycho literature, some four or five hundred numbers, passed to the Royal Library in 1957, five objects having gone to Frederiksborg as his gift of 1950 (Paulli & Dal 1959, pp. 9–10, 15). Grade B: the memorials rest on Mortensen's own article and on his obituarists; no inventory of the Rundetaarn collection or of the stones has been read. The gift of the collection to the state in 1957 was marked by a printed piece, 'Enestående Tycho Brahe samling skænket staten' (De grafiske fag, 1957; Museum Odense DMM/00104x024865). Mortensen's own hand reaches the museums elsewhere: his 1909 pamphlet 'Uraniborg og Stjerneborg Ruiner' inscribed to the poet Thøger Larsen (Lemvig Museum 0135X2453), a photographic enlargement of the SMK drawing sent to Th. Køhl in 1914 (Moesgaard OM0648X0003), and two framed photographs after the Mechanica plates given to Den Gamle By in 1952 (00132:52, 00136:52).

Commemoration after 1946 — the stamp, the planetarium, the 450th year

The quatercentenary of 1946 brought a postage stamp — Museum Odense holds the zinc proof copy of 'nyt frimærke (Tycho Brahe)' (DMM/00101x003112) — and Laurits Nielsen's study of the Uraniborg press, issued for the same anniversary. The four hundredth year of Uraniborg's foundation was marked by Barry Wilmont's relief, 'Uranienborg på halvglobus' with the legend 'T B 1576 Uranienborg 1976', whose working studies are at the Museum of Art in Public Spaces (KØS0131); the University of Copenhagen's 500th anniversary in 1979 by Harald Salomon's plaquette with Tycho's portrait in silver on bronze, one of ten (Museum Sønderjylland 07175X00105; Rasmussen, Harald Salomon, no. 121); and the Queen's tapestries for Christiansborg (1988–92) by Bjørn Nørgaard's border with Tycho's portrait and his instruments beside the Golden Horns (KØS0342) — Nørgaard had already made a sculpture titled 'Uranienborg' in 1974 (Kunsten, Aalborg, NK 1974). The Tycho Brahe Planetarium opened in Copenhagen in 1989 — Des Asmussen's poster of the lit building over the lakes (Den Gamle By 0250X1234), Ole Broager's sculpture Kødet of 1995 (KØS0593), a bronze statuette of Tycho there of which Kroppedal keeps a plaster cast (H2003X0037), and the eclipse glasses of 11 August 1999 that only the planetarium could still supply (Københavns Museum KBM002126X0001). The 450th year of his birth, 1996, was Copenhagen's year as European cultural capital — the 'Tycho Brahe 450 år' posters (Den Gamle By 0279X1842, 0279X1850) — and the Ole Rømer Museum's exhibition 'Tycho Brahe 450 år — den nye stjerne' (1 October 1996 – 13 April 1997, with the Steno Museum; poster 1191X0013, Eckersberg's picture on it), at whose opening the photograph of the Knutstorp miniature was given. His name on ships belongs here too: the Greenland survey vessel M/S Tycho Brahe of 1951 (its radio and Loran sets at the Maritime Museum, 1994:0119–0120) and the DSB ferry Tycho Brahe put on the Helsingør–Helsingborg crossing in 1991. Grade B.

Zeeberg, Urania Titani (1994) — first critical edition of Tycho’s Latin poetry

1994BiographyArecep-zeeberg-urania-titani

Peter Zeeberg’s Tycho Brahes »Urania Titani«: Et digt om Sophie Brahe (Renæssancestudier 7, Museum Tusculanum, Copenhagen, 1994, 320 pp.) is the first critical edition of Tycho’s most ambitious poem and the founding document of Tycho Brahe studies as a branch of Danish literary history. Zeeberg established Tycho’s authorship via the Craig letter (TBOO VII.366), provided the first complete Danish translation, wrote a full literary commentary on the poem’s Ovidian heroide genre, its alchemical and astrological layers, and its use of Uraniborg pseudonyms (Urania / Titan / Apollo / Mercurius), and had the four embedded horoscopes reconstructed by Ove von Späth. The monograph opened a scholarly conversation about Tycho as a humanist-literary figure — distinct from, and complementary to, his reception as an astronomical instrument-maker and observer. The edition was prepared for by Zeeberg’s short 1993 study Den praktiske muse. When the European Space Agency published the results of its astrometric satellite Hipparcos in 1997, the larger of the two star catalogues — 1,058,332 stars with positions good to about a hundredth of an arcsecond — was named the Tycho Catalogue, the smaller and more precise one (118,218 stars, better than a thousandth of an arcsecond) Hipparcos. The data still in use — SN 1572 and the star catalogue

2004–presentScienceAextantrecep-living-data

Tycho's measurements are still working scientific data. His own brightness record of the 1572 new star, reconstructed as a modern light curve (Ruiz-Lapuente 2004), establishes SN 1572 as a normal Type Ia supernova (peak M_V ≈ −19.6) — a classification independently corroborated when the spectrum of the explosion was recovered in 2008 from its centuries-delayed light-echo (Krause et al., Nature), and re-examined from his records again in 2023 (Hinse et al.). His catalogue of about a thousand stars has been machine-read in all three editions and set against the modern Hipparcos positions reduced to epoch 1601 (Verbunt & van Gent 2010): the bulk of his places are good to about 2′ and the best near 1′.

The 2010 exhumation & the death question

2010LegacyBrecep-exhumation-2010

Jens Vellev's Danish–Czech team re-exhumed Tycho in November 2010 and reported in 2012. Their findings bore on two old questions. First, the hair-mercury levels were found too low to be lethal, excluding the acute mercury poisoning that a popular modern theory (some versions accusing Kepler) had alleged; the exhumation thus removed the poisoning hypothesis but did not itself positively establish the cause of death — it leaves standing the traditional account of a fatal bladder ailment / uraemia after the banquet at which Tycho is said to have held his urine too long. Second, a sample associated with the false nose showed a copper–zinc (brass) alloy rather than the gold or silver of legend. The mercury analysis (Rasmussen et al., 'Was He Murdered or Was He Not? — Part I', Archaeometry, 2013; multi-element follow-up Kučera et al. 2017) put the peak at roughly 16 µg/g in the beard-hair about two months before death — far below the 200–800 µg/g of even moderate intoxication, and attributed to Tycho's own alchemical medicine rather than to poisoning. The brass nose was confirmed by the equal-parts copper-and-zinc green stain left on the nasal bones. A 2018 study of the skeleton (Kacki, Velemínský et al., PLoS ONE) found diffuse idiopathic skeletal hyperostosis, supplying a physiological mechanism for the urinary symptoms of the traditional account. (The µg/g peak is reported from the team's own summary of the Archaeometry data — grade B.)

What the numbers are not. The 53 episodes are a selection, not a census of Tycho’s reception. They were entered where the compilation’s own sources — the biographies, the editions, the published exhumation studies, the chancery registers, the Danish journals — documented an episode well enough to grade it; the absence of an episode says nothing about whether it happened, and the number of episodes in a thread measures the compilation’s attention, not the world’s. No figure on this page measures influence. The year an episode begins is the year of its first documented event as the record states it: for the supernova remnant that is the observation of 1572, for the comet reductions the first of the four memoirs. Where a record itself spans two moments (“1655 / 1913–29”) the table prints the numeric bounds and the register prints the record’s own phrase.

Conventions

Dates. Begins is the year of the episode’s first documented event; ends the year of its last, “present” where it continues. The register prints each record’s own date phrase as well, which may name two moments. Old Style and New Style are not distinguished; no episode turns on a day.

Relations. Descends from is the record’s builds on or derived from relation to one of Tycho’s works or manuscripts; connected to gathers the others (a person involved, a place, an object, another episode). Each relation is a link into the pillar that owns the thing.

Record keys. Every record prints its key (recep-kepler-data) and is addressable: #recep-kepler-data as an anchor, or ?episode=recep-kepler-data, which highlights it.

8Desiderata

What this register does not yet hold, what it holds on weaker authority than it would like, and what would settle each point. The list is part of this chapter, not an apology for it.

  1. Riccioli's Almagestum novum (1651) deserves its own record: it is cited here only inside the world-system and crater episodes.
  2. The semi-Tychonic variants: Ursus's scheme of 1588 and Roeslin's of 1597, with Roeslin's comparative plates, are one record since 26 October 2026 (the world systems compared in print); Riccioli's own, and his Almagestum novum, still want a record of their own.
  3. The commemorative record after 1901: the Danish memorials of the 1930s, the monuments before Mortensen (1779–1946) and the commemorations after 1946 are now entered from the Danish museums' records; the quatercentenaries' programmes themselves, the 1971 competition and its plinth, and the Belvedere exhibition of 1971 are not. Vajce's group itself, with Studnička 1901 and the Copernicus year 1973, is entered since 20 October 2026 (the Prague commemoration record).
  4. Kepler's Harmonices mundi (1619) and the third law are not recorded as a separate use of the data; the Science thread jumps from 1609 to 1627.
  5. No online record could be verified for the Týn church's own tomb, for Riccioli's 1651 volumes, for Schjellerup's 1854 memoir, for Bricka's volume of Danske Magazin, or for Zeeberg's two studies; those records carry the citation and no link.
  6. Matiegka's report of 1901 (Bericht über die Untersuchung der Gebeine Tycho Brahe's, Prague 1901) has been read at the page (the house copy, held since 2 October 2026) and the 1901 episode re-cited to it. Still unread: the archaeological report that Matiegka says Herain was preparing for the k. k. Central-Commission in Vienna (printed pp. 4–5); the material in the National Museum's archive that Šimůnek also used; and the joint Herain–Matiegka accounts of 1902, in Czech and in French, that the corpus's bibliographies cite.
  7. Hind's reduction of the comet of 1582 is dated only by the 1846 paper on the comet of 1590; its own place of publication is not recorded here.
  8. The Gingerich citation was resolved by the editor to the 1971 Sky and Telescope article; the compilation's original registry named no work.
  9. Ekdahl's manuscript reports on the 1823-24 excavation, unpublished and un-located in print, would be the only fuller record of what the crypts held when they were opened.
